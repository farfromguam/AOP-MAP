A decorative map border for the 9-patch — six old-school neatlines

One problem, six framings. The map the reader cares about is the 9-patch — but its layers don't all stop at the same line. Land cover, the trail network, water, and the visitor-context callouts each end at different ragged edges, and when the viewport is wide (or the reader scrolls past the data) those mismatched edges trail off into bare paper — it reads as unfinished, not as a map. These mockups wrap the plate in a decorative border that sits off the edge, the way an old survey sheet or atlas plate does: the ornamental margin becomes the visual end of the map, so the ragged data edges read as "inside the frame" instead of "cut off." Every frame renders over the real AOP map (viewer.html at Region zoom) on the live Muted Earth palette, so the comparison is honest. Pick the attributes you want from each — decoration locus, how much text, survey vs. ornament, and how hard the band hides the ragged edge.

V1Ruled & ticked

Survey quadA double-rule neatline with a graticule of minute-ticks down every edge and corner coordinates. Quiet, authoritative — the USGS-quad look. The paper band hides the ragged ends; ticks do the decorating.

V3Filigree corners

Antique plateAn engraved double-line frame with scrollwork flourishes in all four corners and a centered monogram. The vintage copperplate feel. Corner-led, so edge cover is moderate — the band carries it, the curls sell it.

V4Edge text ribbon

GazetteerThe frame is information: title across the top, place & scale across the bottom, coordinates running up the sides. A wide lettered band — directly the "text off the edge" idea, and the strongest, most deliberate ragged-edge mask.

V5Paper mat & vignette

Mounted plateNo ink ornament — a wide museum mat with a bevel, so the map looks recessed below the board and the data simply vignettes into the mat. Quietest and most modern; the cleanest hide, lets the map stay the hero.
